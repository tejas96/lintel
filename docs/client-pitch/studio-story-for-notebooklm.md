# A calmer way to run your studio

A plain-language story of the problem and the proposed solution, for an interior design and
architecture studio. Use this as a source document in NotebookLM, together with the proposal deck.

All names and figures in this document are examples, not real measurements.

---

## 1. A normal day for the owner, today

Imagine a studio with 15 projects running at the same time. One is in concept design. One is
waiting for client approval. One is in 3D visualisation. One is waiting for a site measurement.
One is behind because a designer took unexpected leave.

The owner's day looks like this:

- 09:10 — calls the designers to ask what they are working on.
- 10:30 — reminds someone about the same deadline, for the third time.
- 12:00 — discovers that an important task never started.
- 14:00 — sorts out a leave request on WhatsApp.
- 15:30 — chases a vendor about a delivery that did not arrive.
- 17:00 — a client asks for an update the owner does not have.
- 20:00 — decides tomorrow's work for everyone, again.

The owner has become the system. Every update, reminder and decision passes through one person.
When the owner steps away, the studio slows down.

## 2. What this quietly costs

- **The owner's time.** Hours every week go into asking, reminding and checking, instead of
  growing the studio.
- **Late surprises.** Delays are found when it is too late to fix them without stress or extra
  cost.
- **Team energy.** Priorities are unclear. Good people work hard on the wrong things, then feel
  blamed.
- **Client trust.** Missed dates, slow answers and vendor slips make the studio look less reliable
  than it really is.

None of this is anyone's fault. It is what happens when a growing studio runs on phone calls,
memory and WhatsApp.

## 3. The real problem

Seven parts of the studio do not talk to each other:

1. Priorities — what matters most today.
2. Ownership — who is responsible for each outcome.
3. Project flow — the stages, approvals and deliverables of each project.
4. Visibility — who can see what is happening.
5. Leave — who is away, and what that affects.
6. Vendors and sellers — suppliers, dealers and contractors, their quotes, orders and deliveries.
7. Rewards — how good work is recognised.

Each part lives in a different head, chat or spreadsheet, so the owner connects them by hand,
every day. That is a coordination problem, and it needs a coordination system.

## 4. Our approach: make the right action the easiest action

People will not change their habits just because they get new software. So the software has to
fit how designers, managers and site teams already work. Four principles:

1. **One clear next step.** Everyone opens the app and sees what to do now, and why it matters.
2. **The system explains.** Every warning says what is wrong, what happens next and who should act.
3. **Honest reporting is safe.** Raising a problem early is easier than hiding it, and it is never
   punished.
4. **The owner sees without asking.** The whole business is visible at a glance, without calling
   anyone.

## 5. The solution: seven connected parts, one system

Daily priorities, ownership, project workflow, live visibility, leave and capacity, vendors and
sellers, and fair rewards. All of them use one shared record, so a task, a leave request or a vendor
order is the same thing everywhere. No double entry, one truth for everyone.

## 6. Follow one project from start to handover

We follow one example project, the **Mehta Residence**, through nine steps.

### Step 1 — A new project plans itself

The manager enters the project name, type, client, project manager, delivery date, scope and team.
The studio's own template then creates the full plan: stages, tasks and subtasks, checklists, what
waits for what, suggested dates, who does what, client approvals and deliverables. There are
templates for each kind of work — full residential, commercial, architecture only — and the manager
can add, remove or move anything before the project starts.

### Step 2 — Everyone knows what to do today

Each designer sees a short, ordered plan for the day, with reasons. For example:

1. Finish the living room layout — critical, due 11:00, about 90 minutes. Do this first: the 3D
   renders are waiting for it.
2. Revise the kitchen elevations — high, due 14:00, about 60 minutes.
3. Organise reference images — normal, due tomorrow, about 30 minutes.

The order is not only by deadline. It also weighs what other work is waiting, how important the
project is, and how long each task takes. When things change — urgent work arrives, a client delays
an approval, someone goes on leave, a task takes longer, a deadline moves — the plan updates and
says what changed and why. People can flag a priority that is not realistic.

### Step 3 — Clear ownership, calm follow-up

Every task has one owner, a clear meaning of "done", a deadline, what it waits for, and the next
action. Instead of endless reminders, every task follows the same calm ladder:

1. When assigned — the owner sees the task and accepts the deadline.
2. When work should start — it appears in their plan for the day.
3. When it is at risk — they name the blocker, or ask for a new date with a reason.
4. When a deadline is missed — their manager is told.
5. When a milestone is at risk — the project manager, and the owner if needed, are told.

Waiting for a client, a supplier or a colleague shows as waiting, not as failure. Ownership means
managing the outcome, not taking the blame for every delay.

### Step 4 — The owner's morning, in 30 seconds

The owner opens the system and sees one business health score (for example 87 out of 100) and the
few numbers that matter: projects needing attention, critical work due today, overdue tasks, who is
in and who is on leave, and client approvals waiting.

More importantly, every problem is explained. For example:

> **Project at risk — Mehta Residence.** The 3D renders are due tomorrow. They have not started,
> because the concept still waits for client approval. **Impact:** delivery may slip by two working
> days. **Next step:** follow up on the client approval today. **Who acts:** the project manager.
> **If nobody acts:** the owner gets an alert at 4 PM.

### Step 5 — Client changes, without chaos

After approval, the client asks for a different wardrobe design. The system shows which tasks,
dates and people are affected, and the extra cost. The client confirms the new scope and cost, and
it is recorded. The plan updates and the full history is kept. Changes stop being silent delays;
every change is visible, agreed and on record, which also protects the studio later.

### Step 6 — Leave that protects deadlines

- **The employee asks:** picks the dates, sees the work that falls on those days, prepares a
  handover, and sends the request.
- **The manager decides:** sees who else is away, which deadlines are at risk and who could cover,
  then approves, declines with a reason, or asks for a handover.
- **Emergencies:** the person is marked away at once, their work is listed, possible cover is
  suggested, and nothing moves without approval.

Leave is a normal part of work. The system simply shows its impact early, so nobody has to guess.

### Step 7 — Vendors and sellers, tied to each project

Many project delays start with purchasing. In the new system:

1. **Vendor list** — suppliers, dealers, showrooms and contractors in one place.
2. **Compare quotes** — side by side, item by item.
3. **Purchase order** — approved, then sent.
4. **Track delivery** — expected dates and reminders.
5. **Receive on site** — checked, with photos.
6. **Pay on time** — payments due, in one list.

Each vendor builds a **scorecard** (on-time delivery, quality, price and response), so the studio
knows whom to trust with the next order. **Material approvals** make sure samples and finishes are
approved by the client before anything is ordered. And **delays are honest**: a late delivery shows
as a vendor delay, the designer is not blamed, and the project dates update.

### Step 8 — Site work, done from a phone

A site supervisor opens the project, picks the task, adds site photos, marks the inspection done,
and reports a blocker if there is one. Seconds, not minutes. Vendor deliveries are confirmed on site
in the same way.

### Step 9 — Rewards that feel fair

Example: a milestone reward of ₹2,000 for "Kitchen drawings issued". The system checks that all
deliverables were submitted, the design lead's review passed, the work was on time (or on a new date
that was approved), and the required documents are attached. The reward is then recommended, and
the owner approves it.

What counts: delivery on time, quality, ownership, teamwork, following the process, and
improvement. Everyone can always see how a reward is calculated, what is still pending, why
something was not granted, and how to challenge a wrong record. There are no automatic deductions,
delays outside a person's control never count against them, and rewards can go to one person or to
the whole team.

## 7. Everyone sees what they need — nothing more

- **Owner:** business health, projects at risk, money where tracked, team capacity, decisions waiting.
- **Project manager:** project timeline, milestones and approvals, who does what, client changes,
  vendor orders.
- **Designer:** today's plan, deadlines and files, report a blocker, leave and handovers, recognition.
- **Site supervisor:** site tasks, photos and inspections, deliveries on site, snag list — all on the
  phone.

Budgets, rewards and personal leave reasons are seen only by the right people.

## 8. Smart help — always under the owner's control

1. **Now — clear rules:** reminders, follow-ups, milestone updates and checks on what waits for what.
   Predictable, and always explained.
2. **As history grows — early warnings:** projects likely to slip, people overloaded, estimates that
   are too hopeful, approvals that always run late.
3. **Later — ask in plain words:** for example, "Which projects may miss their dates this month?"
   Answers come from the studio's own records, with the reasoning shown.

The system never approves leave, changes pay, moves deadlines or reassigns work on its own. People
decide. The aim is an AI-assisted studio, not an AI-controlled one.

## 9. Before and after

| Area | Today | With the system |
|---|---|---|
| Who is doing what | Calls and messages | One live view, always current |
| Deadlines | Reminded again and again | Followed up automatically |
| Delays | Found too late | Flagged early, with the reason |
| Leave | WhatsApp and guesswork | Impact shown, handover planned |
| Client changes | Silent disruption | Impact shown, agreed, recorded |
| Vendors | Calls about deliveries | Orders and deliveries tracked |
| Rewards | Based on feeling | Clear rules everyone can see |
| The owner's role | Chasing everyone | Deciding what matters |

## 10. First, we agree the rules — together

Software can make good rules easy to follow. It cannot invent them. In one short workshop we answer
ten questions: Who owns each project? Who decides priorities? Who can assign work? Who can change a
deadline? What does "done" mean? What happens when work is blocked? Who approves deliverables? How
are scope changes agreed? How is leave decided? How are rewards calculated?

## 11. How we build it — five steps, each one ready to try

1. **Discover and agree** — a workshop; the studio's process becomes templates; the rules are written down.
2. **Projects and daily work** — projects from templates, tasks, daily plans, ownership and follow-ups.
3. **Leave and team capacity** — leave with impact checks and handovers; a clear view of workload.
4. **Vendors and rewards** — vendor list, quotes, orders and deliveries; fair reward rules.
5. **Owner view and launch** — business health and early warnings; phone apps, training and go-live.

The studio sees and tries each step as soon as it is ready, so there is no big surprise at the end.

## 12. What the studio gets

A web app for the office, phone apps for iPhone and Android, alerts in the app and by SMS and email
(only when they matter), sign-in with a one-time code on the phone (no passwords), clear access rules
and regular backups, and training and support after launch.

## 13. Next step

Start with one conversation: a two-hour workshop with the owner and team leads, then a map of the
studio's projects, people, vendors and rules, then an agreed plan — and the build begins.

The goal: a studio that runs well, even on the days the owner is not watching.
